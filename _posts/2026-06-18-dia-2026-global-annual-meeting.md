## My DIA Global Annual Meeting 2026 Experience

*This was my second time volunteering at DIA Global Annual Meeting, and it was a rewarding experience. Between the sessions, the exhibit hall, and the conversations with people I had been looking forward to seeing, this conference packed more into three days than I could have anticipated.*

Volunteering at a conference of this scale is genuinely different from attending as a delegate. You are part of the infrastructure. You get to see how the sessions are organized, interact with speakers before and after they present, and meet fellow volunteers who are equally passionate about regulatory science.

This year, I had the chance to reconnect with fellow volunteers and DIA community members including Julia, Jessica, and Sorcha McCrohan. I also got to meet and spend time with Alberto Grignolo, and Chris Slawecki. Both bring decades of experience and a perspective on this industry that you simply cannot get from a textbook.

I also stopped by the Vivpro Corporation booth to reconnect with former colleagues Pravin, Joga, Holly, and Jessica, and attended their session *AI in Action: Scaling Regulatory Document Generation Across R&D*. Angela Winnier shared her firsthand experience using the REGAIN platform for CSR authoring, a grounded, practical take on what AI-assisted regulatory writing actually looks like day to day.

Beyond that, the exhibit hall and sessions brought a lot of other reconnections, running into people from across the industry, and regulators from FDA and EMA, was a reminder of just how interconnected this field really is.

### Hidden Potential: Turning Today's Science into Tomorrow's Cure

The conference opened with a plenary that set the tone for everything that followed. *David Fajgenbaum (Co-Founder and President, Every Cure) delivered the keynote, moderated by Audrey Greenberg (Venture Partner and Chair, Mayo Ventures, Mayo Clinic), with a panel that brought together Rahul Gupta (President, GATC Health), Dave Pfeiffer (Senior Vice President, Head of ARCAS Bio-Tech, Acrisure Re), Greg Sarafin (Chief Administrative Officer, Sovereign AI), and Lawrence Tallon (Chief Executive Officer, MHRA).*

The central question was one that anyone in drug development grapples with: how do we turn what we already know into cures that actually reach patients? And increasingly, the answer the panelists kept returning to was AI, applied thoughtfully and at the right points in the pipeline.

<div class="pv-definition-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">Computational Pharmacophenomics</div>
  <p>David Fajgenbaum used this session to introduce Computational Pharmacophenomics, a field that combines traditional drug repurposing (one disease, many candidate drugs) with indication expansion (one drug, many diseases)), and scales both computationally across all drugs and all diseases simultaneously. The platform trains AI models on biomedical knowledge graphs encoding known drug-disease relationships, protein interactions, phenotypic features, genes, and pathways, generating predicted treatment scores for every possible drug-disease combination across 74 million pairs. The first patient treated using a top-scoring compound has been in remission for over three years, with findings published in <em>The New England Journal of Medicine</em>.</p>
</div>

Several broader themes ran through the panel discussion:

<div class="pv-common-grid">
  <div class="pv-common-card">
    <div class="pv-common-card-title">Reducing drug failure</div>
    <p>AI applied to efficacy and safety evaluation could meaningfully lower the rate of drug failure enabling better go/no-go decisions earlier in the pipeline.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pv-common-card">
    <div class="pv-common-card-title">The value chain is changing</div>
    <p>AI is removing the historical shortage of inputs that slowed development. Automation is handling work that previously required significant manual effort, and providers across the ecosystem are becoming <em>super providers</em> organizations that can take on broader scope because AI tools have extended their capabilities.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pv-common-card">
    <div class="pv-common-card-title">Capital and distribution</div>
    <p>AI can de-risk the science of drug discovery enough to attract investment, and the conversation centered on making sure that value actually reaches patients.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pv-common-card">
    <div class="pv-common-card-title">Asking the right questions</div>
    <p>AI gives you the ability to ask better questions of larger datasets. But good AI-assisted decision-making starts with good scientific and regulatory thinking, not the technology itself.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pv-rule-callout">

  <p>Lawrence Tallon's presence as CEO of MHRA engaging openly with a conversation about AI's role in de-risking drug development was itself a signal of how much the landscape has shifted.</p>
</div>

### AI in Regulatory Review and Decision Making: Perspectives from FDA, EMA, MHRA, and PMDA

This was the session I had been most looking forward to. What made it different from most AI-in-regulatory panels was who was sitting at the table: the actual regulatory scientists and policy leads from four of the world's most influential health agencies, speaking openly about how AI is being adopted, evaluated, and governed within their own organizations.

<div class="pv-compare-cols">
  <div class="pv-compare-col pv-col-left">
    <div class="pv-compare-col-title">FDA</div>
    <ul>
      <li>AI tools do not make regulatory decisions, they support the human reviewer</li>
      <li>Internal AI review team evaluating digital tools for submission review workflows</li>
      <li>All tools validated using predefined methodologies, free from bias</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="pv-compare-col pv-col-right">
    <div class="pv-compare-col-title">PMDA (Japan)</div>
    <ul>
      <li>No jurisdiction-specific standardized expectations for AI use in submissions yet</li>
      <li>Sponsors must proactively define the role of AI in their processes</li>
      <li>Final review accountability rests unambiguously with human experts</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pv-rule-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">On Sponsors Using Generative AI to Write Submissions</div>
  <p>The answer from all agencies was unified. The underlying principles of data integrity do not change based on how the document was generated. Accuracy, completeness, and traceability remain the governing standards. What AI introduces as a distinct new requirement is <strong>transparency</strong>. Sponsors are expected to clearly disclose where and how AI was used, particularly within risk management frameworks. The tool changes; the responsibility does not.</p>
</div>

<div class="pv-rule-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">EMA and MHRA: EU AI Act Implications</div>
  <p>EMA and MHRA are actively mapping AI use cases in drug development against the EU AI Act risk classification framework. The EU AI Act does not replace GxP principles; it layers additional governance requirements on top of them, around transparency, traceability, and human oversight of AI-assisted outputs.</p>
</div>

**Cross-Agency Harmonization and the GAIP Principles**

FDA and EMA are actively collaborating on joint guidance for AI use in drug development. The two agencies have established a joint framework: the Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice (GAIP) in Drug Development, a 10-point program that spans the entire medicines lifecycle, from early discovery and clinical trials to manufacturing and post-market safety, to ensure AI tools are safe, transparent, and compliant with global GxP standards. Consistency across agencies is being pursued through systematic validation of AI prompts, LLM training alignment, and structured frameworks ensuring AI-generated outputs meet regulatory-grade standards.

In January 2026, FDA's CDER and CBER jointly published, together with EMA, the Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice (GAIP) in Drug Development, a concrete output of the cross-agency harmonization work the panel was discussing.

### Threading AI Throughout the Drug Development Lifecycle: An Enterprise Lens

Brandon Rice (CEO and Co-Founder, Weave Bio) and Andrew Robertson (VP, Head of Global Regulatory Policy and Innovation, Takeda) presented one of the most practically grounded sessions of the conference. This was not a theoretical discussion about AI potential. It was a detailed look at what actually happened when a major pharmaceutical company committed to using AI across its regulatory submission workflow.

<div class="pv-definition-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">The Weave Platform</div>
  <p>An AI-native cloud software designed to unify and drive regulatory workflows in drug development, from source to submission. Built around three core principles: <strong>outcome-oriented</strong> (spanning the full source-to-submission lifecycle), <strong>comprehensive real-time collaboration</strong> (human and AI working together across authoring, review, studies, and CMC), and <strong>AI-native for regulated R&D</strong> (source-grounded generation, end-to-end traceability, human accountability, and automatic verification).</p>
</div>

**Pre-IND Tox Summaries**

The challenge in IND authoring is familiar: heavy information lookup across thousands of pages of source study reports, inconsistent structure across pharmacology, PK, and tox reports, and slow initial drafting that creates a bottleneck.

### Parexel x Weave Bio: The Connected Submission

Katie Connelly (SVP Head of Regulatory Affairs, Parexel) and Brandon Rice (CEO, Weave Bio) made a bigger structural argument about how AI transformation in regulatory actually works.

<div class="pv-rule-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">The Fragmentation Problem</div>
  <p>The regulatory submission lifecycle is still highly fragmented. Wwork happens in silos across planning, content creation, review, assembly, and submission. The instinct is to apply AI to fix that. But their argument was sharper: <strong>AI does not fix fragmentation. It scales it.</strong> If your processes are siloed and manual, applying AI just means you produce siloed, manual outputs faster and at greater volume.</p>
</div>

The sequence that actually works:

<div class="pv-workflow">
  <div class="pv-workflow-steps">
    <div class="pv-workflow-step">Standardization</div>
    <div class="pv-workflow-arrow">›</div>
    <div class="pv-workflow-step">Automation</div>
    <div class="pv-workflow-arrow">›</div>
    <div class="pv-workflow-step">AI-Enabled Authoring</div>
    <div class="pv-workflow-arrow">›</div>
    <div class="pv-workflow-step pv-workflow-final">Intelligent Review</div>
  </div>
  <p class="pv-workflow-note">Throughout all stages, human expertise remains in the lead: scientific and regulatory judgment, interpretation of regulatory context, and critical review.</p>
</div>

<div class="pv-section-label">Takeaways</div>

## What I Am Taking Forward

Every conference gives you information. The good ones give you perspective. DIA 2026 gave me both.

<div class="pv-rule-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">The Through-Line</div>
  <p>AI in regulatory science is real, it is accelerating, and the agencies are actively engaged with it, not just as something they regulate, but as something they themselves are adopting. The January 2026 FDA-EMA GAIP publication is proof that cross-agency harmonization on AI governance is not just a conversation. It is being formalized.</p>
</div>

<div class="pv-rule-callout">
  <div class="pv-callout-label">What Has Not Changed</div>
  <p>The consistent message from FDA, EMA, MHRA, and PMDA was clear: data integrity, traceability, human accountability, and transparency are not legacy concepts that AI will supersede. They are the standards that AI must meet.</p>
</div>

<div class="pv-definition-callout">
  <p><em>"Don't let perfect be the enemy of good. Start somewhere. Experiment. Iterate. Much more is learned, much faster, if you have your own hands in the mix."</em></p>

</div>

That is the energy I am carrying into the rest of 2026.

Grateful to DIA for creating a space where this kind of learning and this kind of community is possible.
